UX WALKTHROUGH · DRAFT FOR ALIGNMENT

Bob's half of this design is a blank page. The other half is the whole job.

Walkthrough 15 drew a receipt: a step 6 where every shared row reads Ready and nothing carries a button. This walkthrough is what makes that receipt true. On Bob's side it adds nothing, not a screen, not a button, not a word he has to learn. Everything it adds lands on the administrator, once, and on a small service nobody in accounting will ever hear of. Both halves are drawn here, because a design that shows only the empty one has hidden its own price.

The job, stated as a job: when my department's tools need a key I don't have and shouldn't have, I want the work to just run, so I can close the month without asking a colleague to DM me something I know I'm not supposed to have.

NEW marks a capability today's product does not have. Nothing here is ratified: the design is at review after two security passes, and screen 13 is the list of things only the owner can settle. Bob's screens name no vendor, no protocol and no component; they describe effects, because that is all he can act on. The administrator's screens name things precisely, because precision is what she is owed. Every quoted string is CLI authored: the app renders the sentence, it never composes it (invariant #1).

The store records the scope, not the person.

Per-person attribution lives only in the membership service's log, and joining the two takes the machine id and a time window. That is the one genuine advantage the rejected shape had, and it should be said out loud whenever the two are compared, rather than discovered during an incident.

The one alarm worth wiringNEW

An authentication at the store with no matching line in the membership service's log means something signed an assertion that was not the service. That is the only available signal for a stolen signing key, because a forged assertion never touches the service at all.

And every refusal, with its reason

Denials are logged as carefully as grants. That is what lets the CLI say "your team hasn't made this available to you" and "I couldn't reach the check" as two different sentences to two different people, instead of one shrug to both.

Nothing here is a value. Only decisions.

Log hygiene is a design requirement, not an implementation detail

  • The second security pass found that the design never said secret-shaped values must be absent from these logs. Nothing here, and nothing in the support affordance on screen 04, may carry an assertion, an access token, or a GitHub token, at any verbosity. Cheap on day one, expensive after a log pipeline exists.
  • The same pass sharpened the detection story: a service whose logic was tampered with writes a log that looks perfect, because the tampered code is what writes it. The periodic check therefore has to compare the service's decisions against GitHub's own record, not only against the store's configuration.
  • If per-person attribution inside the store ever becomes a hard requirement, the mitigation is one identity per person rather than per scope. It is sketched in the design and deliberately not chosen, because it puts an object with a lifecycle back into a design whose whole thesis is that there is not one.

PART THREE · WHAT THIS DESIGN DOES NOT CLAIM

12

The structural claim, and the two duties it does not remove

Transparency
STRUCTURAL, AND NARROW

The service cannot read a key, and two things can still go wrong that it will never see

The claim is worth making because it is structural rather than careful: the service holds nothing the store would accept as a credential, so it cannot create a scope, widen one, or read anything at all. Compromise it and an attacker impersonates entitled people for a window. They do not empty the store. That claim has edges, and stating it without them would turn it into marketing.

01Impossible by construction, not by care. The service can only assert claims that identities the administrator already created are already bound to. The shape the brief literally described, a service that creates credentials, can create a scope, widen one and read the store, because the power to create an identity is the power to grant it access.
02Nothing long lived, and nothing store identifying, is at rest on the Mac. The corrected version of an overclaim the design made and the second pass caught. What is cached is a short lived access token, one scope, expiring on its own, never renewable locally.
03Revocation needs no revocation. Every read requires a fresh assertion and every assertion requires a live membership check. There is no standing grant anywhere to find and remove.
04It holds two secrets, of unequal severity. Its own signing key, which can forge assertions the store will accept, and the GitHub App key, which can only read team rosters. The design originally claimed one. That was wrong, and it is corrected rather than quietly dropped.
05Edge one: the signing key. NEW Whoever holds it forges any scope for any person, offline, indefinitely, until it is rotated, and never touches the service, so the service's own log shows nothing. Highest score in either pass, at 7.4. Custody, the ninety day rotation, and the store-side alarm on screen 08 are the whole answer.
06Edge two: the name. NEW Whoever controls DNS for the service's address can stand up an impostor with a valid certificate, and the store itself will go and fetch the impostor's keys. This defeats rule 01 without ever touching the service. Registry lock, DNSSEC and multi-factor on the registrar account are prerequisites, not hygiene.
07One vendor question is still open. Whether the store caches those fetched keys, and for how long, decides how long an impostor's window stays open. Nobody has asked. Somebody must, before this ships.
08The console is the root. Whoever administers the store can repoint the trust with no review anywhere in the loop. 7.0, and the largest single point of failure in the architecture.
09The mapping is the boundary. Whoever can push one file widens access across the organization on the next pull. 6.6, verified live twice, and the cheapest fix on the ruling list.
10The sign-up endpoint is a membership oracle. Rate limits and identical answers for "not a member" and "no such person" reduce roster enumeration. They do not remove it, and that residual is stated rather than solved.
11Two GitHub accounts, one person, breaks the one act claim. Removal is one act per account. The offboarding list has to enumerate accounts, not people. Low score, real silent failure.
12Read the scores honestly, not mechanically. The two edges land in the middle band because compromising a registrar or a build pipeline is hard, not because the damage is smaller. Both max the damage column. High consequence, medium likelihood, and never a lower priority than a compromise of the service itself.
→None of this argues for the alternative. Every finding on this list is present and worse in the shape that creates credentials, which carries all of them plus one component whose compromise is the store's compromise.
Two passesSeventeen findings scored across an independent second review: none critical, three high, nine medium, five low. The second pass corrected two overclaims in the design's own headline and found one missing instruction, which is on screen 04.
Not ratifiedThe verdict is "survives, with changes." The changes are screen 13.

Overselling is the failure mode here

  • Stated without its edges, the structural claim becomes a slogan, and the first person to find the DNS path would be right to distrust everything else in the document.
  • Two of these are operational duties with owners and cadences rather than code. They belong on the ruling list precisely because a designer cannot assign them to anyone.
  • One is an unanswered vendor question. Writing "unconfirmed" next to it is not a weakness in the design. Pretending otherwise would be.

PART FOUR · THE RULINGS

13

Eight things only the owner can settle

Decisions
DECISION NEEDED · OWNER

None of these are design questions, and none can be answered by building more

Each carries a recommendation and a consequence that follows from choosing differently. Every one of them changes something a person feels, or something a person has to remember.

1 · Where it livesBeside the store, on the organization's own host. Name who holds that host's secret manager, and who holds the deploy credential: whoever can ship a build to it decides what gets asserted, without ever touching the key.
2 · Fifteen minutesHow long a shared key stays valid. Longer is more resilient to an outage and slower to revoke, by exactly the same amount. One number decides both, and it is the number on screens 04 and 05.
3 · Ninety daysRotation on a schedule, immediately on suspicion, with an overlap longer than any shared key's life so a rotation stays invisible.
4 · Who can widen accessRequire the second administrator, by turning off the exemption or by a rule on that path. Do not ship without one of them. It is the cheapest fix here relative to its score, and the only item on this list about a system that already exists.
5 · Thirty daysClean evidence before the organization-wide login is revoked. It spans a monthly close, which is the work this design exists to protect.
6 · Console accountsHardware-key multi-factor, named individuals only, in place before the console is used to create the first identity rather than after.
